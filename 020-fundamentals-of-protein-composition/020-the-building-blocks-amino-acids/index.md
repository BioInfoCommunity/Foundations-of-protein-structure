# The Building Blocks: Amino Acids

As mentioned, proteins are extraordinarily diverse molecules, built from a conserved set of 20 standard amino acids. For this section, it is important to remember that amino acids are physical objects that occupy real space (albeit space we can’t see).

Understanding amino acids is therefore essential for understanding how proteins fold, how they function, and how changes in sequence can alter biological behaviour. In proteins, amino acids are linked together into long polypeptide chains. Once incorporated into a chain, an amino acid can also be referred to as a **residue**.

Each individual amino acid has both conserved features shared by all amino acids and unique chemical properties that influence protein structure and function.

## **General structure**

While the 20 amino acids have different properties, they all share a common chemical framework. Each amino acid has a common core structure that consists of a central carbon, C𝞪 (the alpha carbon). Attached to this central atom are four distinct groups:

1. An amino group (-NH2, or -NH3+ under physiologicalpH).
2. A carboxyl acid group (-COOH, or -COO– under physiological conditions).
3. A hydrogen (H) atom.
4. A variable side chain, also denoted as the R-group.

The central 𝞪-carbon is bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and a variable side chain (R).

The amino and carboxyl groups, along with the Cα, form the “**backbone**” of the protein. This backbone is a repeating, universal scaffold that runs the length of the protein, while the R-groups project outward like branches on a tree. It is the specific chemistry of these R-groups that gives each amino acid its diversity and ultimately determines how the protein will fold.

## **Amino acid nomenclature and chirality**

Amino acids are commonly represented in one of two ways:

* **Three-letter abbreviations** (such as Ala for alanine, or Gly for glycine), or
* **Single letter codes** (A or G).

These shorthand systems allow long protein sequences to be written, read, and analysed more efficiently. For example, the sequence of a small protein might be written as:

MKTIIALSYIFCLVFA…

or

Met-Lys-Thr-Ile-Ile-Ala-Leu-Ser-Tyr-Ile-Phe-Cys-Leu-Val-Phe-Ala…

You will encounter both systems in the literature and databases. The single-letter code is more compact and thus preferred for long sequences, while the three-letter code is often clearer when discussing specific residues in detail.

As a side note, with the exception of glycine (whose side chain is a simple Hydrogen), all amino acids are chiral molecules, meaning they exist as non-superimposable mirror images, much like a left and right hand. Biological proteins are built exclusively from L-amino acids, which all share the same stereochemical arrangement at the Cα. This uniform “handedness” is not arbitrary; it ensures that protein chains fold in a consistent and predictable way.

One consequence is that many common protein features also have a preferred handedness; for example, α-helices in natural proteins are mostly right-handed.
