# Primary structure: Why sequence matters

The primary structure of a protein is simply its amino acid sequence, the linear order of amino acids from N-terminus to C-terminus. While this might seem like the simplest level of protein structure, it is arguably the most important because:

* Every protein is defined by a unique sequence of residues, and all subsequent levels of organisation (secondary, super secondary, tertiary and quaternary) rely on this primary level of structure.
* The sequence determines the protein’s chemical properties, folding pathway, and final function.
* A sequence is what we can easily read from genomic data.

## **Anfinsen’s dogma: Sequence determines structure**

In the 1960s, Christian Anfinsen performed a series of landmark experiments on ribonuclease A. He chemically denatured the protein, disrupting its structure, and then allowed it to refold under appropriate conditions. Remarkably, the protein spontaneously returned to its native, functional three-dimensional conformation. From these experiments emerged what is commonly known as **Anfinsen’s dogma**: the amino acid sequence of a protein contains all the information necessary to specify its native structure.

## **The significance of sequence in bioinformatics**

For researchers, the primary sequence is often the first, and sometimes the only, data available about a protein. Understanding how to extract information from sequences is therefore essential across multiple domains:

* If a sequence contains a conserved motif, a researcher can predict the function, even before solving its structure.
* Sequence comparison allows us to reconstruct the evolutionary history of a protein. High sequence identity between two proteins suggests homology, implying they diverged from a common ancestor.

Not all positions in a sequence are equally structurally important. Through multiple sequence alignments (MSAs), we can identify conserved residues—those that remain unchanged across millions of years of evolution.

In terms of similarity, it is important to remember the difference between:

* **Sequence identity:** The percentage of positions with identical residues (e.g., 60% identity).
* **Sequence similarity:** The percentage of positions with identical or chemically similar residues (e.g., Asp→Glu or Ile→Leu).

However, structure is more conserved than sequence. Proteins with as little as 15% sequence identity can have nearly identical folds. And we will explore this later.
