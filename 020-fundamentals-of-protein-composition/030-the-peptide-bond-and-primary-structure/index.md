# The peptide bond and primary structure

Amino acids are linked together to form proteins through a **condensation reaction,** in which the carboxyl group of one amino acid (residue n) reacts with the amino group of the next amino acid (residue n+1). This reaction releases a water molecule and forms a covalent bond between the carbonyl carbon and the nitrogen atom of the amine group: this is known as a peptide bond. Repetition of this reaction generates a linear chain of amino acids, and the specific order of residues in this chain defines the **protein’s primary structure.**

Formation of the peptide bond. A condensation reaction between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next releases water and forms a covalent peptide bond (C-N). This linkage creates the continuous backbone of the polypeptide chain.

## Polypeptide chain directionality

Each polypeptide has two chemically distinct ends. The N-terminus (amino terminus) contains a free α-amino (NH2) group. By convention, this residue is labelled residue 1. The C-terminal (carboxyl terminus) contains a free α-carboxyl (COOH) group. Protein sequences are always written, read and synthesised in the N-terminus to C-terminus direction (N → C). Between these two termini, there can be any number of amino acids connected by a peptide bond, in a repeating pattern of …N-Cα-C-N-Cα-C….
