Side chain chemistry

While the backbone of every amino acid is identical, the chemical properties of the R group (side chain) vary widely. A range of terms is used to describe side chains, reflecting differences in:

  • Physical size: e.g. small or bulky
  • Conformational flexibility: e.g. rigid or flexible
  • Physiochemical properties: such as being electrically charged, hydrophilic, polar, hydrophobic, non-polar, acidic or basic.

These differences dictate where residues prefer to sit in a folded protein, how proteins interact with other molecules and whether mutations disrupt structure or function.

Residues are grouped by the chemical properties of their side chains. The chart includes 3-letter and 1-letter codes along with the chemical structure of the R-group.

For clarity, we group amino acids into broad physicochemical classes. These categories are simplifications; many residues fit more than one description,  but they are extremely useful for structural reasoning.

Hydrophobic (non-polar) side chains do not interact favourably with water. They tend to cluster together to avoid the aqueous solvent, much like oil droplets in a glass of water.  This tendency to bury nonpolar groups away from water is called the hydrophobic effect. It is a major driving force for folding; we will return to why it is so powerful (in thermodynamic terms) in the next section when we discuss how whole proteins collapse and stabilise their folds.

  • Hydrophobic amino acids include: alanine (Ala A); valine (Val, V); isoleucine (Ile, I); leucine (Leu, L); methionine (Met, M); phenylalanine (Phe, F); tyrosine (Tyr, Y); tryptophan (Trp, W).
  • Alanine has a very simple side chain: a single methyl group (CH3), which is chemically inert.
  • Isoleucine and leucine, as their names suggest, are isomers differing in the arrangement of methylene and methyl groups.
  • Phenylalanine (Phe, F); tyrosine (Tyr, Y); tryptophan (Trp, W) are special hydrophobic residues that contain an aromatic structure, that is a carbon ring.
  • Although tyrosine and tryptophan are classified as hydrophobic, they also contain polar functional groups, making them amphipathic (i.e. having both hydrophilic and hydrophobic properties). As a result, they are often found at the interface between the hydrophobic core and the surrounding solvent (the aqueous environment a protein lives in)
  • Methionine (Met, M) contains a flexible sulphur-bearing chain. Though classified as hydrophobic due to its hydrocarbon backbone, the sulphur provides some polarity. Methionine serves as the universal start codon for protein synthesis (AUG), meaning that every protein begins with methionine, though it is often removed post-translationally.

Polar, uncharged side chains can form hydrogen bonds but do not carry a net electrical charge at physiological pH. Due to their ability to interact favourably with water, polar, uncharged residues are often found on the surface of proteins where they contribute to protein solubility and stability. They are also frequently located within binding pockets or active sites, where hydrogen bonding is critical for molecular recognition and catalysis.

  • Residues in this class are: serine (Ser, S); threonine (Thr, T); asparagine (Asn, N); and glutamine (Gln, Q).
  • One of the most important reactions to a serine or threonine side chain is the addition of a phosphate group to create phosphoserine or phosphothreonine.  This occurs as a post-translational modification in the cell after protein synthesis on the ribosome and is important in protein–protein interactions and intracellular signalling pathways.
  • Cysteine (Cys, C) contains a thiol (–SH) group that can form disulphide bonds with other cysteine residues. When two cysteines are close in the structure, the two sulphur atoms will form a covalent bond of similar strength to the peptide bond between sequential amino acids. Disulphide bonds are much stronger than non-covalent hydrogen bonds, and they play an important role in stabilising the three-dimensional structure of many proteins, particularly those that function outside of the cell.

This schematic illustrates the formation of a disulphide bond, a covalent linkage between two cysteine residues. Each cysteine contains a reactive thiol group (–SH) in its side chain. When two cysteines come into close proximity in an oxidising environment, their thiol groups lose hydrogen atoms and form a sulphur–sulphur (S–S) bond. This covalent bridge links either two regions of the same polypeptide or two separate chains, stabilising the three-dimensional structure of the protein.

Charged amino acids have side chains that carry a net positive or negative charge. These residues interact strongly with water and are therefore most commonly found on the surface of proteins where they contribute to solubility and molecular interactions. Charged residues can be divided into positively charged (basic) and negatively charged (acidic) side chains.

  • Positively charged (basic) amino acids include lysine (Lys, K): arginine (Arg, R); and histidine (His, H). Lysine and arginine are almost always positively charged at physiological pH, while histidine can exist in either a charged or uncharged state, allowing it to play important roles in pH-sensitive interactions and enzyme catalysis.
  • Negatively charged (acidic) amino acids include aspartic acid (Asp, D); and glutamic acid (Glu, E), which carry a negative charge due to their carboxylate-containing side chains.
  • Electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged residues, often referred to as salt bridges, help stabilise protein structures and are important in protein-protein interactions and ligand binding.

The special cases: these amino acids have unique structural effects that often dictate the path of the polypeptide backbone (e.g., Cysteine, Proline, Glycine).

  • Glycine (Gly, G) is the simplest amino acid; it has a single hydrogen atom (which is much smaller than a carbon) as its side chain, making it the smallest amino acid.  It is achiral and extremely flexible. The absence of any significant functional group means that it possesses little intrinsic chemical reactivity. For structure interpretation, glycine is often enriched in tight turns and flexible hinges, and it can appear in conformations that would be sterically impossible for most other residues.
  • Proline (Pro, P) is structurally unique because its side chain forms a cyclic ring by bonding back to the amino nitrogen, creating a secondary amine (an imino acid). This rigid structure restricts backbone flexibility and often introduces bends or kinks in protein structures, frequently disrupting regular secondary structures such as α-helices

Importantly, these properties are not mutually exclusive, and individual amino acids are often described using multiple descriptors. For example, Tryptophan (Trp, W) is defined by its fused indole ring system, which classifies it as a bulky, aromatic amino acid. While its carbon skeleton is inherently non-polar, the secondary amine within the ring provides a dipole that allows it to function as a hydrogen bond donor. It is normal for this terminology to feel unfamiliar at first, and developing comfort with the language used by protein biologists comes with time and practice.
