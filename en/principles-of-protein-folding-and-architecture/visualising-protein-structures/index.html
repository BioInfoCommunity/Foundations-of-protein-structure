Visualising protein structures

Before exploring how proteins are organised, it is important to understand the tools structural biologists use to view them.

A protein structure file (such as a PDB or mmCIF file) is essentially a large text file containing the X, Y, Z coordinates for every atom in the protein. These coordinates are typically derived from experiments such as X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), though they can also be generated by computational predictions. In addition to atomic coordinates, structure files contain relevant metadata regarding the relative “certainty” of atomic positions.

However, a list of atomic coordinates is not intuitive to interpret. To make sense of this information, we use different graphical representations. Each representation highlights particular features of the molecule, and the choice of representation depends on the question being asked. For example, are you interested in the precise geometry of chemical bonds, the overall shape of the protein, or how it might interact with another molecule? The question you are trying to explore will inform the visualisation that is appropriate.

At the most detailed end of the spectrum are the atomic (or ball-and-stick) representations, which show precise individual positions of individual atoms and their covalent interactions. However, a model of an entire protein, with every atom displayed, can easily become visually overwhelming due to the sheer amount of information. It is often impossible to interpret the overall shape or see the relationship between distant parts of the protein chain in this representation.

To understand the protein’s organisation, we must simplify. One common approach is the cartoon representation, which traces the path of the polypeptide backbone and smooths away most atomic detail. This representation emphasises recurring patterns in the backbone that will be introduced in the next section. By reducing visual clutter, it becomes much easier to see the overall architecture and how different regions of the chain are arranged relative to one another.

At the other end are surface representations, which display the overall molecular shape and provide a simplified view based on the collective physiochemical properties of the amino acid. We can map chemical properties onto this surface. For example, colouring by electrostatic potential or hydrophobicity allows us to predict how the protein interacts with ligands or other proteins.

It is critical to remember that these macromolecular models are not perfect copies of reality, but rather representations derived from this data. Just like any model, their accuracy and reliability can vary significantly. Experimental structures typically report how well the model fits the data, while predicted structures (e.g., from AlphaFold) include confidence estimates that can help you decide which regions are likely reliable for inference.
