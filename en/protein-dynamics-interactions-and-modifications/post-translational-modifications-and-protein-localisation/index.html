Post-Translational Modifications and protein localisation

Post-Translational Modifications (PTMs)

The information in a gene (the amino acid sequence) is not always enough to define a protein’s function. Once synthesised, some proteins are subject to a vast system of chemical “edits” and “shipping labels,” the addition of which is controlled by a huge suite of enzymes. This is called ‘post-translational’ modification. This is the primary way a cell regulates the function of its proteins in real time, much faster than the hours or days it takes to transcribe and translate a new protein.

Chemical groups are chemically conjugated to the protein, predominantly on specific amino acid side chains, by enzymes that have evolved to do this task. The UniProt PTM list catalogues more than 600 different types of modifications. based on the UniProt Knowledgebase controlled vocabulary (Release 2026_01).

The most common modifications are:

  1. Phosphorylation, where phosphate groups are added to serine, threonine, tyrosine (and rarely histidine and aspartate).
  2. Glycosylation, where chains or one or more carbohydrate groups are covalently bonded to asparagine or serine and threonine.
  3. Ubiquitination, where a small protein called ubiquitin is attached to lysine residues. This modification is used to signal that the ‘tagged protein’ needs to be disposed of.
  4. Acetylation, where an acetyl group is added to lysine. This modification is used commonly to control DNA packaging and is important for gene regulation.
  5. Methylation, where a methyl group is added to lysine or arginine. Similar to acetylation this modification is important for DNA packaging and gene regulation.

Post-translational modifications can alter the structure and/or charge of a protein and may even change the interactions the protein can mediate. There are other forms of post-translational modification that can also alter the stability and, in some cases, the tertiary structure of folded protein. Some of these modifications, such as phosphorylation and N-acetylation, are reversible and can act as conformational switches.

Signal peptides and localisation

Signal peptides are short 16–30 amino acid sequences typically found at the N-terminus of proteins. Standard signal peptides contain a defined cleavage site for signal peptidase. Less commonly, signal peptides occur at the C-terminus and feature non-canonical cleavage motifs.

These signals play a key role in directing nascent peptides to the secretory pathway and determining their final localisation in the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, plasma membrane, or extracellular space.

After translocation, signal peptides are typically removed by signal peptidases, enzymes that cleave them.
