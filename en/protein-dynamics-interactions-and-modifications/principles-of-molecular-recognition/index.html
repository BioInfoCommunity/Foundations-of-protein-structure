Principles of molecular recognition

The function of most proteins is realised through their interactions with other molecules. Whether signaling a cell to divide, transporting an ion across a membrane, or catalysing a metabolic reaction, the process begins with a physical encounter between two surfaces, the binding interface.

To understand how these interactions drive biological processes, we must examine the principles of complementarity, the thermodynamics of weak interactions, and the chemical nature of the surfaces involved.

The “fit” between a protein and its binding partner, whether that partner is a small molecule (ligand) or another macromolecule, is governed by complementarity. This concept extends far beyond simple geometric shapes. True complementarity exists when:

  • Geometric complementarity means the physical contours of the binding surfaces allow them to approach closely, with protrusions on one surface fitting into depressions on the other.
  • Chemical matching: Hydrogen-bond donors on one surface are positioned opposite acceptors on the other; nonpolar groups align with other nonpolar groups; and positive charges are paired with negative charges.

Complementarity is necessary because an intermolecular interface is composed of many weak interactions. Any single hydrogen bond or van der Waals interaction is transient at body temperature. However, because free energies are additive, tight binding can be achieved through a large number of weak interactions. The strength of binding is maximised when contact surfaces fit closely together, ensuring that:

  • All possible van der Waals contacts are made
  • hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors at the interface between the two molecules pair with each other instead of making hydrogen bonds to water
  • The combined energy of multiple weak interactions overcomes the entropic cost of bringing two molecules together

Complementarity ensures that a protein “recognises” its specific partner amidst a crowded cellular environment of thousands of other molecules.

Specificity vs Affinity

Although related, specificity and affinity are distinct properties that are often confused.

  • Specificity is about what a protein binds, its ability to discriminate between similar molecules
  • Affinity is the strength of a protein’s interaction with its partner.

Importantly, specificity does not necessarily mean a protein binds tightly. Proteins may discriminate between two ligands perfectly (high specificity) but still release the bound molecules (this is called moderate affinity). Many proteins rely on a balance of specificity and affinity to function; examples include many enzymes and signalling proteins that need to bind tightly enough to be selective and weak enough to allow reversibility. A protein can exhibit high specificity without high affinity.

Affinity

Affinity describes the total strength of an interaction, the sum of all weak non-covalent forces holding two molecules together. We quantify this using the dissociation constant (Kd): a lower Kd indicates a tighter (higher affinity) interaction. Biological interactions span an enormous range, from millimolar (weak) to femtomolar (extremely tight).

Interestingly, biological systems are often composed of complexes with moderate affinity (micromolar to nanomolar). This allows for regulation and turnover. Too tight binding can inhibit dynamic control and affect signaling, transport and enzyme catalysis. For drugs, affinity has practical consequences. A drug with low affinity would need to be used at higher concentrations, increasing the risk of off-target effects on other protein systems.
