Binding site

Macromolecule recognition

The specific recognition of a macromolecule by a protein usually involves interactions over a large contiguous surface area (hundreds of square Ångstroms) or over several discrete binding regions. The macromolecule makes many points of contact with the protein’s surface, and these interactions provide substantial binding energy.

Consequently, accurately predicting macromolecule-binding sites on a protein surface is challenging. The most frequently observed sites are protruding loops or large cavities because these provide specific shape complementarity, but relatively flat binding sites also exist. Many binding sites for RNA or DNA involve protruding loops or α-helices that fit into the major and minor grooves of the nucleic acid. Interestingly, these protrusions often lack obvious common features and may not adopt the same structure in the unbound protein as when the ligand is bound.

Small molecule binding sites

Many biologically important ligands are small molecules: enzyme substrates, cofactors that contribute to catalysis, and allosteric effectors that modulate enzyme activity from remote sites. Such small molecules typically bind at deep pockets or clefts on the protein surface, except in certain cases when they are buried within the protein interior.

Deep binding pockets offer several advantages:

  • They allow the protein to envelop the ligand, using shape complementarity to provide specificity
  • They hinder water access to the bound ligand, which can be important for many enzyme reactions
  • They can provide unusual microenvironments (very hydrophobic, highly charged, etc.)
  • They enable even a small molecule to make enough contact points for strong binding

This characteristic means binding sites can often be identified even in unliganded protein structures.

Access to buried sites

When a ligand binds in an interior cavity, it must be able to diffuse through the protein structure. Protein structural flexibility facilitates this penetration, even for relatively large substrates. Computer simulations suggest that there can be multiple pathways by which a ligand can “worm” its way into a buried cavity. Alternatively, the enzyme may exist in open and closed states, with substrate binding to the open form triggering a conformational change (domain or flap closure) that sequesters the active site.
