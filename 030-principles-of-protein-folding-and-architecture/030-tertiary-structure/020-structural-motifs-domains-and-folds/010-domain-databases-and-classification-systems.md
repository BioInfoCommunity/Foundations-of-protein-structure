# Domain databases and classification systems

Given the central importance of domains to protein structure and function, several major databases have been developed to catalogue and classify them. Each takes a somewhat different philosophical approach, but all aim to organise the universe of protein domains into understandable families.

Structure-based classification systems organise domains by their three-dimensional architecture rather than sequence similarity. **CATH (Class, Architecture, Topology, Homology)** is a hierarchical system that groups domains at multiple levels. At the broadest level, domains are classified by their secondary structure composition at 4 levels:

* **Class**: Domains share the same secondary structure composition (alpha, beta, mixed).
* **Architecture**: Domains with the same arrangement of secondary structure segments.
* **Topology**: Domains with the same arrangement and connectivity of secondary structure segments.
* **Homology**: Domains with evidence for an evolutionary relationship.

**SCOP (Structural Classification of Proteins)** follows a similar hierarchical philosophy but emphasises evolutionary relationships and expert curation. Both CATH and SCOP reveal an important principle: there are far fewer protein folds than there are protein sequences. While evolution has generated millions of different protein sequences, these sequences adopt only a few thousand distinct structural folds. This conservation of structure over evolutionary time reflects both the constraints of protein physics; only certain architectures are stable; and the success of certain structural solutions that have been reused and adapted for many different functions.

This hierarchical classification helps researchers identify structural and functional relationships between proteins, even when sequence similarity is too low to detect directly.

As mentioned earlier, proteins can be composed of multiple domains with cooperative functions, and domain recombination is a major source of natural functional diversity. This modular architecture enables the evolutionary creativity we observe across the tree of life.

This classification scheme is not just academic. When you determine or predict the structure of a new protein, recognising its structural class immediately suggests possible functions and guides your analysis.
