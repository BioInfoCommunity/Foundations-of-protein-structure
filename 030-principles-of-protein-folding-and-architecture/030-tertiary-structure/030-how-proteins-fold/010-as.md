# The folding funnel: Energy landscape theory

Modern understanding of protein folding is captured by the energy landscape theory, often visualised as a folding funnel. Imagine a three-dimensional landscape where the vertical axis represents free energy and the horizontal axes represent all possible conformations.

At the top of this funnel sits the unfolded state: a random coil with high energy and high entropy because many conformations are accessible. As folding proceeds, the protein descends the funnel toward lower energy states.

The funnel is not smooth. Its sides are rugged, with local dips and bumps representing metastable intermediate states. Proteins do not follow a single, defined pathway down the funnel. Instead, multiple parallel pathways converge toward the bottom. As the protein folds, secondary structures form first, helices and sheets stabilised by local hydrogen bonding. These elements then coalesce and pack together, with hydrophobic side chains consolidating into the core. Along the way, the protein may pass through a molten globule state: a compact intermediate with secondary structure in place but with internal side chains still fluid and not yet locked into their final positions.

The bottom of the funnel represents the global energy minimum: the native, functional structure. This is the most stable state under physiological conditions. However, this state is only marginally stable, typically by about 20-60 kJ/mol, only about 10-20 times the thermal energy available at body temperature. This marginal stability is not a weakness but a feature: it ensures proteins are not rigid statues but dynamic entities that can be regulated, undergo conformational changes for function, and be degraded when no longer needed.

The rugged landscape means proteins can get stuck in local energy minima, kinetic traps where they adopt partially folded, non-native structures. If a protein remains trapped with hydrophobic patches exposed, it may aggregate: multiple misfolded proteins stick together through their exposed hydrophobic regions, forming clumps that can be toxic to cells. To prevent this, cells employ molecular chaperones: proteins that recognise and bind to exposed hydrophobic patches on folding proteins, shielding them from aggregation and providing repeated opportunities to fold correctly. Some chaperones, such as GroEL/GroES, even provide an isolated chamber in which a protein can fold without competition from other cellular components.

In thermal environments above absolute zero (0° K), we see local, rapid atomic motions. Bond lengths and angles vibrate, methyl groups rotate, and solvent-exposed side chains and loops “wiggle” or “thrash.” These thermally driven fluctuations are large enough to allow small molecules, such as water, to penetrate the protein’s interior.

This thermal motion is not just random noise; it is often essential for function, as it allows the protein to explore different micro-states on its energy landscape. To understand a protein’s true biological function, how it works in the dynamic, crowded, and fluctuating environment of a living cell, we must reintroduce three crucial concepts: proteins move, they interact, and they are regulated.

A traditional atomic model of a protein is often perceived as a static, rigid snapshot. However, to understand a protein’s true biological function within the dynamic and crowded environment of a living cell, we must reintroduce three crucial concepts: proteins move, they are chemically regulated, and they rarely act alone.

Function arises from a protein’s ability to navigate a complex energy landscape. Sometimes these movements are subtle wiggles; at other times, entire domains shift dramatically, akin to the opening and closing of a door. Many disease-causing mutations, for instance, don’t disrupt the fold but rather subtly alter this energy landscape, making a non-functional state too stable or a functional one too hard to reach.
