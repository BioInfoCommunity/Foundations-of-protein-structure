# How proteins fold

One of the most profound questions in molecular biology is how a linear chain of amino acids spontaneously folds into a specific three-dimensional structure. This question encompasses both mechanisms: what physical forces drive folding, and kinetics; how folding happens so quickly despite astronomical numbers of possible conformations.

## **Levinthal’s paradox and Anfinsen’s dogma**

In 1969, Cyrus Levinthal pointed out a fundamental mystery: if a small protein of 100 residues were to sample every possible conformation randomly, it would take longer than the age of the universe to find its native state ([Levinthal, Cyrus., 1969)](https://web.archive.org/web/20110523080407/http://www-miller.ch.cam.ac.uk/levinthal/levinthal.html). However, most proteins fold into their native structures in a matter of milliseconds, suggesting that the folding process samples only a very small fraction of all possible conformations before reaching its most stable state.

The resolution to Levinthal’s paradox came from experiments by Christian Anfinsen in the early 1970s. Anfinsen showed that the enzyme ribonuclease, when completely unfolded by harsh chemical treatment, could spontaneously refold into its native, active state upon return to physiological conditions, without any external template or machinery. This demonstrated that all the information needed to specify the three-dimensional structure is encoded in the amino acid sequence itself. This principle, now known as Anfinsen’s dogma, states that under physiological conditions, a protein will spontaneously seek its most thermodynamically stable state: the global energy minimum ([Anfinsen, 1973](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.181.4096.223)).

The fact that the folding instructions are intrinsic to the sequence explains why we can often successfully express human proteins in non-native systems (for example, a mammalian protein expressed in *E. coli*), as long as the chemical environment is similar, the sequence will drive the same fold.

## **The hydrophobic effect**

Protein folding is an autonomous process driven by the quest for thermodynamic stability. As a polypeptide chain emerges from the ribosome, it transitions from a high-entropy random coil (a disordered state with many possible shapes) to a low-entropy native conformation (the functional 3D shape).

The primary driver is the hydrophobic effect. Much like oil droplets separating from water, hydrophobic residues cluster together in the protein core to avoid contact with the surrounding solvent, while hydrophilic residues remain solvent-exposed on the surface. This separation of chemically distinct side chains produces a compact structure in which helices, sheets and loops pack together to maximise favourable interaction and minimise energetic penalties. This hydrophobic effect causes the polypeptide to become compact.

From an energetic point of view, the compactness produces two favourable results:

* it minimises the total hydrophobic surface area in contact with water, and
* it brings the polarisable hydrophobic groups close to each other, allowing van der Waals interactions between them.

It has been shown that, for several proteins, smaller intermediate folding structures appear as metastable states on the folding path, such as molten globules ([Ptitsyn, O., 1995](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-3233(08)60546-X)). For different proteins, very different pathways have been observed experimentally.
