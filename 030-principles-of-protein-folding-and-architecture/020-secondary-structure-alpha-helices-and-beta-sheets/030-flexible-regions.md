# Loops, turns, and connecting elements

α-helices and β-sheets cannot account for the complete structure of a protein. These regular elements must be connected by less structured regions that allow the polypeptide chain to change direction and pack into complex three-dimensional arrangements. These connecting regions, loops, turns, and random coils are often dismissed as mere linkers, but they frequently play crucial functional roles.

**Loops** are stretches of a polypeptide chain that connect one secondary structure element to another (e.g. connecting an α-helix and a β-sheet). Loops typically range from a few to twenty or more amino acids that connect secondary structure elements. Because they are not constrained by the regular hydrogen bonding patterns of helices or sheets, loops are often more flexible and solvent-exposed. These elements are often critical for facilitating protein-protein interactions (as discussed in the “[Protein dynamics, interactions, and modifications](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/training/online/courses/foundations-protein-structure/protein-dynamics-interactions-and-modifications/)” section). Loop regions that connect two adjacent antiparallel β-strands are called hairpin loops.

**Turns** are particularly short loops, usually only three to five amino acids long, that facilitate sharp changes in chain direction. The most common is the β-turn, which typically appears at the end of a β-strand and allows the chain to fold back on itself. This enables two adjacent strands to align antiparallelly. When a β-turn connects two antiparallel β-strands, the resulting structure is called a β-hairpin. These hairpins are common building blocks in larger β-sheet structures.

**Random coils**, more accurately called disordered regions, lack stable, defined structure on their own. They sample many different conformations and do not appear well-defined electron density in crystal structures. Some proteins have large disordered regions that remain flexible even in the folded protein, and in some cases, entire proteins are intrinsically disordered. We will return to the functional importance of disorder later, but for now it suffices to note that disorder is not equivalent to lack of function. Many disordered regions become structured upon binding a partner, providing a mechanism for highly specific yet adaptable molecular recognition.
