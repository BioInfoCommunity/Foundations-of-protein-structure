# Secondary structure: α-helices and β-sheets

Secondary structure elements come from regular repeating arrangements of the polypeptide backbone that are primarily stabilised by hydrogen bonds between the carbonyl oxygen of one amino acid and the amide hydrogen of another. While the amino acid backbone is universal, different R-groups tend to prefer different secondary structure conformations.

These repeating patterns are more than decorative: they are the building materials that assemble into domains and folds, and they strongly influence which mutations are structurally tolerated.

To understand why certain backbone conformations are so prevalent, we must first understand the hydrogen bond, the interaction that directly defines the geometry of alpha-helices and beta-sheets.

## **Hydrogen bo****nding**

A hydrogen bond forms when a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to an electronegative atom, most commonly nitrogen (N-H) or oxygen (O-H), is attracted to a second electronegative atom nearby. To picture why this happens, imagine that electrons in a molecule are not perfectly shared. Atoms like oxygen and nitrogen are “electron-hungry”: they pull the shared electron cloud towards themselves, leaving the hydrogen atom with a slight positive charge. This electron-depleted hydrogen is then attracted to a lone pair of electrons on a neighbouring nitrogen or oxygen, which carries a slight negative charge. The result is a weak but directional electrostatic attraction: the hydrogen bond. Geometry matters; the interaction is strongest when the donor atom, hydrogen, and acceptor atom are roughly collinear, and weakens as this angle deviates.

A single hydrogen bond is relatively weak. But the backbone of every amino acid contains both a hydrogen bond donor (the amide N–H) and a hydrogen bond acceptor (the carbonyl C=O), giving the polypeptide chain an intrinsic capacity to form a dense, repeating network of hydrogen bonds. It is precisely this network that drives the formation of alpha-helices and beta-sheets: individually modest interactions that, when aligned into a regular pattern, create remarkably stable and well-defined structures.

It is energetically very unfavourable not to form a hydrogen bond, because it would leave one or more partial or full charges uncompensated. Thus, in protein structure, nearly all potential hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors are participating in such interactions, either between polar groups of the protein itself or with water molecules.

It is also worth noting that hydrogen bond strength is context-dependent: a backbone H-bond buried in a helix contributes more energetically than one at a solvent-exposed surface, where water molecules compete for the same donor and acceptor groups.
