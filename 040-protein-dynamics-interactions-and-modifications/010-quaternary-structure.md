# Quaternary structure

Proteins rarely act alone. Their functions are almost always defined by their interactions with other molecules, including other proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and small molecules. The quaternary structure refers to the assembly of two or more individual polypeptide chains (subunits) into a larger, functional complex.

This level of organisation opens up functional possibilities that are impossible for monomeric proteins. Quaternary structure allows the formation of catalytic or binding sites at the interface between subunits, creates opportunities for cooperative behaviour and regulation, and enables proteins to form large macromolecular machines with multiple activities.

## **Oligomeric assemblies: homomers and heteromers**

Protein assemblies composed of more than one polypeptide chain are called oligomers, and the individual chains are termed monomers or subunits. These assemblies can contain two (dimers), three (trimers), four (tetramers), or even more subunits.

Some oligomers contain only one kind of monomer, while others are made up of two or more different chains:

* Oligomers composed of only one type of monomer are prefixed homo, for example, homotrimer.
* Oligomers composed of different monomers are prefixed hetero; for example, hemoglobin, which contains two alpha and two beta chains, is a heterotetramer.

The number and kinds of subunits in an assembly, together with their relative positions in the structure, constitute the quaternary structure of an assembly.

**Diversity in protein quaternary structure.**
Protein oligomers are classified by the number and type of their constituent subunits.
Homomers (e.g., homotrimer a3,
homopentamer a5) consist of identical polypeptide chains, often displaying high rotational symmetry.
Heteromers (e.g., heterotrimer abc, heterotetramer abcd) are composed of distinct subunits, allowing for complex interfaces and specialized functional regulation.

## **Symmetry in protein assemblies**

Protein assemblies built of identical subunits are usually symmetric.  This isn’t coincidental; it’s a geometric necessity. For identical subunits to interact, each must possess a binding region and its complementary binding region. The most efficient way to achieve this is through symmetric arrangements where each subunit occupies an equivalent position relative to its neighbours.

So powerful is the tendency toward symmetry that it even influences oligomers composed of nonidentical polypeptide chains. Many of these proteins are pseudosymmetric, as seen in hemoglobin and the proteasome, where slight differences between subunits create an almost, but not quite, symmetric arrangement.

The irregular surfaces of proteins enable them to bind specific ligands and associate specifically with other proteins; this surface chemistry is what underlies the formation of quaternary structure.
