# Intrinsically disordered proteins and regions

The classic “sequence → structure → function” paradigm implies that a stable three-dimensional fold is a prerequisite for function. We now know this picture is fundamentally incomplete. A substantial fraction of the human proteome (perhaps 30-40%) consists of proteins or protein regions that lack stable tertiary structure under physiological conditions and possess a high degree of conformational flexibility, yet remain biologically active ([Intrinsically Disordered Proteins: An Overview](https://www.mdpi.com/1422-0067/23/22/14050)).

When an entire protein lacks a stable structure, it is referred to as an Intrinsically Disordered Protein (IDP). However, in most cases, only specific segments within an otherwise structured protein are disordered and functional; these are called Intrinsically Disordered Regions (IDRs). Whether we’re discussing IDPs or IDRs, these disordered elements are not misfolded or damaged; their lack of fixed structure is a critical functional feature that has endowed proteins with what we might call “functional promiscuity”: the ability to interact with multiple different partners and participate in diverse biological processes.

IDRs exist as a dynamic “ensemble” of interconverting conformations rather than adopting a single stable structure. This flexibility is encoded in their amino acid composition: IDRs are enriched in charged and polar residues (Lys, Arg, Glu, Gln, Ser, and Pro) and depleted of hydrophobic residues. Without sufficient hydrophobic residues to form a stable core, these regions remain dynamic and solvent-exposed, providing conformational plasticity.

This plasticity allows IDRs to adapt their shape to bind many different partners, often in mutually exclusive ways. Some IDRs can assume a specific structure when they bind a partner protein, a process called coupled folding and binding, which represents another form of induced fit. Post-translational modifications (PTMs) also enable IDPs/IDRs to attain diverse conformations, thus increasing the total repertoire of structures resulting from an IDP/IDR sequence.

Examples of functionally important IDRs include transcription factors (such as the p53 transactivation domain), signaling proteins (such as kinase activation loops), and RNA-binding proteins (such as FUS and hnRNPA1).
